Box Breathing

Four equal steps: in, hold, out, hold. Four breaths a minute. The technique most widely taught in military and emergency service training.

5 min read

Four seconds in, four held, four out, four waiting. Then from the top.

Four equal sides. The name comes from the shape: draw it on paper and you get a square.

What it is good for

The two holds bring the breath down to four a minute. That is less than half the resting rate of a calm person. Slowness itself is a signal in the body: someone breathing at this rate isn't running and isn't being chased.

Its second job, and perhaps the more important one, is about attention. You have to count all four steps and can't let any of them slide. There is no gap left for the mind to wander into.

This technique is widely taught in military and emergency service training. The reason isn't complicated physiology but its simplicity: easy to count, doable standing up, no equipment needed.

Where the evidence stands

There are few controlled studies done directly on box breathing. The best comparison we have is the 2023 study described in the article on the physiological sigh: box breathing was there as a one-month practice arm and showed improvement in mood measures — behind the sigh, but ahead of where it started.

The general effects of slow breathing apply to this technique too. Claims beyond that — "resets your stress", "doubles your focus" — don't come from the studies.

How to do it

Sit upright, feet on the floor. Breathe in through the nose for four seconds, letting the belly rise.

Hold for four seconds. Don't lock your throat, just stop.

Breathe out through the nose for four seconds — not by blowing, but quietly and evenly.

Wait four seconds with empty lungs. This is the hardest step. If waiting is uncomfortable, shorten the length.

Common mistakes

Lifting the shoulders on the inhale. The breath should go to the belly, not the chest. Put a hand on your belly: let that be what rises.

Locking the throat during the hold. Closing the airway creates pressure and makes the hold harder. Just stop.

Struggling with the second hold. Waiting with empty lungs feels hard to most people. If you struggle, work on Triangle Breathing first — that second hold isn't there.

How the app measures it

There are two separate hold windows and both are measured separately. It shows in the report as hold cleanliness: in how many cycles no air escaped.

The most common leak is in the empty hold — a small amount of air seeping out when the breath isn't finished. This usually comes from ending the exhale early instead of spreading it across the four seconds.

Who should take care

If you get dizzy during a hold, skip it. Long holds can be uncomfortable for people with blood pressure problems; shortening the length or choosing a technique without holds is better.
